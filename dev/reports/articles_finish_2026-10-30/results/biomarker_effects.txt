Biomarker Treatment Effects in the Weibull AFT/Cox Framework

Theory, Estimands, and Causal Foundations

ForestSearch Package Documentation

2026-10-30

Source: vignettes/articles/biomarker_effects.qmd

1 Introduction

This vignette develops the mathematical framework for biomarker-dependent treatment effects used in the ForestSearch simulation machinery. The central idea is a piecewise-linear spline model embedded in a Weibull regression that is simultaneously an accelerated failure time (AFT) model and a Cox proportional hazards model. This dual representation allows treatment effects to be specified as causal log-hazard-ratios on the Cox scale while survival times are generated on the AFT scale.

Three treatment effect estimands are defined for summarizing biomarker-modulated heterogeneity:

-   the Average Hazard Ratio (AHR) — a geometric-mean summary on the log-hazard scale,
-   the Controlled Direct Effect (CDE) — a ratio of average hazards on the natural scale (Aalen, Cook & Roysland, 2015),
-   the Marginal (causal) HR — a population-level Cox estimate from stacked potential outcomes.

The first two are deterministic functions of the data-generating mechanism (DGM); the third serves as a stochastic validation target. Their theoretical relationships, conditions for agreement and divergence, and causal standing are developed below.

This DGM family is the one behind the published simulation studies. León and Anderson (2026a, Section 7) evaluate the post-selection correction on an accelerated-failure-time super-population built from the GBSG data by create_gbsg_dgm() and generate_aft_dgm_flex() (their Supplementary Section S9), and 2026b (Section 5.1) builds its survival design from the same trial’s covariate structure, censoring and event rates.

2 The Weibull AFT/Cox Model

2.1 Weibull Distribution Essentials

For the Weibull distribution with shape parameter ν > 0 and scale parameter θ > 0, the density, survival, hazard, and cumulative hazard functions are:
$$\begin{aligned}
{f(t)} & {= \nu\,\theta^{-\nu}\, t^{\nu - 1}\,{\exp}\!\left( - (t/\theta)^{\nu} \right),} \\
{S(t)} & {= {\exp}\!\left( - (t/\theta)^{\nu} \right),} \\
{\lambda(t)} & {= \nu\,\theta^{-\nu}\, t^{\nu - 1},} \\
{\Lambda(t)} & {= (t/\theta)^{\nu}.}
\end{aligned}$$

For ν ∈ (0,1) the hazard is strictly decreasing; for ν > 1 it is strictly increasing. A useful distributional identity is that the cumulative hazard evaluated at the random variable T is unit-exponential:

Probability-Integral Property.
Λ(T) = (T/θ)^(ν) ∼ Exp(1).
This follows because Pr (Λ(T)≤t) = Pr (T≤Λ⁻¹(t)) = F(Λ⁻¹(t)) = 1 − e^(−t).

2.2 The Dual AFT / Cox Representation

Writing Q = Λ(T) ∼ Exp(1) gives log Q = ν(logT−logθ), which rearranges to the AFT form:

log (T) = log (θ) + τ ε,

where τ = 1/ν is the AFT scale parameter and ε = log Q has the standard extreme-value distribution with density f_(ε)(x) = exp (x−e^(x)).

Incorporating a covariate vector L, the Cox (hazard) parameterization is:
λ(t; L) = λ₀(t) exp (L^(′)β),
and the corresponding AFT parameterization is:
log (T) = log (θ) + L^(′)γ + τ ε,
where the two coefficient vectors are linked by the AFT-to-hazard transformation:

AFT / Hazard-Scale Transformation.
β = − γ / τ.
R’s survreg estimates (logθ̂, γ̂, τ̂) on the AFT scale; hazard-ratio coefficients β̂ are obtained via (4).

This transformation is the linchpin of the framework: potential outcomes are simulated on the AFT scale (3), while treatment effects are interpreted on the hazard-ratio scale via β.

3 Biomarker-Dependent Treatment Effects

3.1 The Two-Phase Spline Model

To induce treatment effects that vary with a continuous biomarker Z, the Cox linear predictor is specified as a piecewise-linear spline with a single interior knot at Z = k:

L^(′)β := β₁ X + β₂ Z + β₃ Z X + β₄ (Z−k) 1(Z>k) + β₅ (Z−k) 1(Z>k) X,

where X ∈ {0, 1} denotes the treatment indicator. The five terms have the following roles:

  --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Parameter  Term             Role
  ----------- ---------------- ---------------------------------------------------------------
      β₁      X                Main treatment effect (intercept of log-HR)

      β₂      Z                Prognostic biomarker effect (both arms)

      β₃      ZX               Biomarker × treatment interaction (slope of log-HR for Z ≤ k)

      β₄      (Z−k)1(Z>k)      Spline term: change in prognostic slope above k

      β₅      (Z−k)1(Z>k) X    Spline term: change in treatment-effect slope above k
  --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.2 The Causal Log-Hazard-Ratio Function

Under the potential outcomes framework, let λ_(x, z)(t) denote the hazard function for a subject with biomarker level Z = z had they followed treatment X = x. The causal log-hazard-ratio at biomarker level z is:

ψ⁰(z) := log  (λ_(1, z)(t) / λ_(0, z)(t)) = β₁⁰ + β₃⁰ z + β₅⁰ (z−k) 1(z>k).

Because the baseline hazard λ₀(t) cancels in the ratio, ψ⁰(z) is free of t — a direct consequence of the proportional hazards structure conditional on Z. Expanding (6) piecewise:

$$\psi^{0}(z) = \begin{cases}
{\beta_{1}^{0} + \beta_{3}^{0}\, z,} & {z \leq k,} \\
{\beta_{1}^{0} + \beta_{3}^{0}\, z + \beta_{5}^{0}\,(z - k),} & {z > k.}
\end{cases}$$

3.3 Anchor-Point Parameterization

Rather than specifying (β₁⁰,β₃⁰,β₅⁰) directly, the framework specifies the log-HR at three interpretable anchor points — ψ⁰(0), ψ⁰(k), and ψ⁰(ζ) for some ζ > k — and solves:

$$\beta_{1}^{0} = \psi^{0}(0),\qquad\beta_{3}^{0} = \frac{\psi^{0}(k) - \psi^{0}(0)}{k},\qquad\beta_{5}^{0} = \frac{\psi^{0}(\zeta) - \psi^{0}(0) - \beta_{3}^{0}\,\zeta}{\zeta - k}.$$

Verification. These formulas are verified by substitution. For instance, evaluating ψ⁰(ζ) from (6) gives ψ⁰(ζ) = β₁⁰ + β₃⁰ ζ + β₅⁰(ζ−k); solving for β₅⁰ recovers the third expression in (7) exactly. The same check holds at z = 0 and z = k, confirming internal consistency.

3.4 Connection to Potential-Outcome Means

There is an important link between the log-HR ψ⁰(z) and the conditional means of the potential log-survival times. Define m(x,z) := E[log T ∣ Treat = x, Z = z] under the AFT model (3). Then:

$$\psi^{0}(z) = \frac{m(0,z) - m(1,z)}{\tau}.$$

Derivation. On the AFT scale the treatment-related terms in log (T) contribute L^(′)γ =  − τ(L^(′)β) to m(x,z). Hence the difference in conditional means between control (x = 0) and treatment (x = 1) at biomarker level z is:

m(1,z) − m(0,z) = γ₁ + γ₃ z + γ₅ (z−k) 1(z>k) =  − τ ψ⁰(z),

which gives ψ⁰(z) = {m(0,z) − m(1,z)}/τ as required. This confirms that ψ⁰(z) measures the causal difference in expected log-survival between the two treatment arms, scaled by τ.

  Note

  Sign Convention. Throughout this vignette, ψ⁰(z) < 0 means treatment is beneficial (HR  < 1), while ψ⁰(z) > 0 means treatment is detrimental (HR  > 1). This follows the standard Cox model convention where negative log-HR favors the experimental arm.

3.5 Extension to Prognostic Factors

When an additional baseline prognostic factor W is included, the linear predictor becomes:

l_(x, z, w) = β₁⁰ x + β₂⁰ z + β₃⁰ zx + β₄⁰ (z−k) 1(z>k) + β₅⁰ (z−k) 1(z>k) x + β_(w)⁰ w.

The prognostic term β_(w)⁰ w does not interact with treatment, so the causal log-HR remains:
$$\psi^{0}(z,w) = \frac{m(0,z,w) - m(1,z,w)}{\tau} = \psi^{0}(z),$$
i.e., it depends on z but not on w. When prognostic factors do interact with treatment, ψ⁰ would depend on (z,w) jointly, and the AHR/CDE definitions below would average over both.

3.6 Working Example

A representative configuration for an oncology biomarker setting:

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Anchor    z              ψ⁰(z)                 HR Interpretation
  -------- ----- --------------------------- ------- ----------------------
    Low      0         log (3) ≈ 1.10           3.00 Strongly detrimental

    Knot     5        log (1.25) ≈ 0.22         1.25 Modestly detrimental

    High    10       log (0.5) ≈  − 0.69        0.50 Strongly beneficial
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------

This profile produces a biomarker-modulated treatment effect that transitions from harm at low biomarker levels to substantial benefit at high levels, crossing the null (HR  = 1) between z = 5 and z = 10. The overall average hazard ratio across the full biomarker distribution is approximately 0.74, indicating net benefit in the population.

4 Treatment Effect Estimands

Three estimands are used to summarize treatment effects across biomarker-defined sub-populations. Each captures a different aspect of treatment effect heterogeneity.

4.1 Average Hazard Ratio (AHR)

The biomarker AHR is the exponentiated mean of ψ⁰(Z) across a biomarker-threshold sub-population:

AHR(z⁺) := exp  {E_(Z ≥ z) ψ⁰(Z)},   AHR(z⁻) := exp  {E_(Z ≤ z) ψ⁰(Z)}.

The AHR is the geometric mean of the individual causal hazard ratios exp (ψ⁰(Z_(i))) across the sub-population. In ForestSearch’s super-population data, this is computed from the loghr_po column as $\text{AHR}(\mathcal{S}) = {\exp}\!\left( {\overline{\text{loghr\_po}}}_{\mathcal{S}} \right)$.

Key properties.

-   Deterministic: depends only on the model coefficients and covariate distribution, not on the random error ε_(i).
-   Reproducible: identical across simulation replications for a fixed super-population.
-   Dual causal status: as a geometric-mean ratio of potential-outcome hazards, the AHR achieves both individual-level and population-level causal interpretability under the Fay & Li (2024) taxonomy. This is because log  linearizes the ratio, so E[log (HR_(i))] = E[log λ_(1, Z)] − E[log λ_(0, Z)], and the order of comparison and summarization is irrelevant.

4.2 Controlled Direct Effect (CDE)

The CDE is the ratio of average exponentiated log-hazards under treatment versus control (Aalen, Cook & Roysland, 2015):

$$\text{CDE}(z^{+}):=\frac{{\bar{\theta}}^{1}(z+)}{{\bar{\theta}}^{0}(z+)},\qquad\text{CDE}(z^{-}):=\frac{{\bar{\theta}}^{1}(z-)}{{\bar{\theta}}^{0}(z-)},$$

where θ̄^(x)(z+) = E_(Z ≥ z) exp (l_(x, Z)) and l_(x, z) is the Cox linear predictor at treatment x and biomarker z. In ForestSearch, this is computed from the theta_1 and theta_0 columns: $\text{CDE}(\mathcal{S}) = {\overline{{\exp}(\theta_{i}(1))}}_{\mathcal{S}}\;/\;{\overline{{\exp}(\theta_{i}(0))}}_{\mathcal{S}}$.

Key properties.

-   Deterministic (like the AHR): no dependence on ε_(i).
-   Natural-scale averaging: by averaging on the hazard (exponential) scale rather than the log-hazard scale, the CDE gives more weight to subjects with larger absolute hazard contributions.
-   Differs from AHR under heterogeneity: Jensen’s inequality implies CDE(𝒮) ≠ AHR(𝒮) in general, with the discrepancy growing as within-subgroup treatment-effect variability increases.

4.3 Marginal (Causal) Hazard Ratio

The marginal HR for a subgroup 𝒮 is obtained by fitting a Cox model to stacked potential outcomes — a dataset where each subject contributes two rows (one under treatment, one under control), both with event indicator 1:

HR_(marg)(𝒮) = exp (β̂_(Cox)),

where β̂_(Cox) is the coefficient from coxph(Surv(time, event) ~ treat) fit to the stacked data within 𝒮.

Key properties.

-   Stochastic: depends on the realized error terms ε_(i), introducing Monte Carlo variability across simulation replications.
-   Population-level: the Cox partial likelihood implicitly weights subjects by their risk-set contributions, targeting a population-averaged effect.
-   Validation role: serves as the DGM-level ground truth against which per-replicate estimator bias is assessed.

4.4 When the Three Estimands Agree — and When They Diverge

Under a constant treatment effect (no heterogeneity), the individual log-HR is identical for all subjects, and all three measures coincide:
HR_(marg) = AHR = CDE = exp (β_(treat)).

Under heterogeneous treatment effects, three sources of divergence arise:

1.  Jensen’s inequality separates AHR from CDE: ${\exp}\!\left( \overline{{\log}\text{HR}_{i}} \right) \neq \overline{{\exp}(\theta_{i}(1))}\;/\;\overline{{\exp}(\theta_{i}(0))}$ unless all log HR_(i) are identical.

2.  Cox partial-likelihood weighting separates the marginal HR from the AHR, because risk-set membership creates implicit non-uniform weights.

3.  Monte Carlo variability affects the marginal HR (which depends on ε_(i)) but not the AHR or CDE.

The AHR is the recommended primary estimand for simulation studies because it is deterministic, directly interpretable as a geometric-mean HR, and achieves dual causal status. The marginal HR serves as a validation target, and the CDE provides a complementary natural-scale perspective.

5 Causal Foundations

5.1 Why the AFT Model Serves as the Causal DGM

Both Aalen, Cook & Roysland (2015) and Fay & Li (2024) identify the AFT model as a natural causal foundation for survival analysis. The Weibull AFT scale-change parameter exp (−β_(treat)) satisfies:
exp (−β_(treat)) = γ₁/γ₀,
where γ_(x) is the geometric mean of the potential survival time T_(i)(x). Crucially, this geometric-mean ratio is simultaneously:

-   Individual-level causal: it equals exp  (E[logT_(i)(1)−logT_(i)(0)]), with comparison formed within individuals before averaging.
-   Population-level causal: it is identifiable from marginal distributions in a randomized trial.

This dual status — unique among common survival ratio estimands — is why the ForestSearch DGM is built on the Weibull AFT. The biomarker log-HR ψ⁰(z) = {m(0,z) − m(1,z)}/τ inherits this property at each biomarker level.

5.2 The AHR as a Causally Valid Functional

The ForestSearch AHR is defined from individual potential-outcome log-HR differences:
$$\text{AHR}(\mathcal{S}) = {\exp}\!\left( \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum\limits_{i \in \mathcal{S}}\lbrack\theta_{i}(1) - \theta_{i}(0)\rbrack \right),$$
where θ_(i)(x) = X_(i)(x)^(′)β₀. Because this is a geometric mean of individual causal log-hazard differences, it falls within the class of estimands that achieves both individual-level and population-level causal status under the Fay & Li (2024) taxonomy.

This property is not shared by the instantaneous Cox HR e^(β̂), which suffers from conditioning-set distortion at each event time (the “collider” argument of Hernan 2010; Aalen et al. 2015; Martinussen 2022): among survivors at time t > 0, the treatment and frailty are no longer independent even in a randomized trial.

5.3 Proportional Hazards and Marginal Non-PH

The Weibull DGM generates data satisfying proportional hazards conditional on the biomarker. Under conditional PH, the Cox HR is a valid population-level causal estimand (Fay & Li 2024; Beyersmann, Schmoor & Schumacher 2025). However, marginally — averaging over the biomarker distribution — PH need not hold when treatment effects are heterogeneous. This is precisely why the framework reports AHR and CDE (marginal summaries) rather than a single Cox HR as the primary characterization of biomarker-modulated treatment effects.

The Abrahamowicz et al. (2025) simulations further support this design: frailty-induced bias in Cox HR estimates is substantial only under very strong unmeasured susceptibility. Since the ForestSearch DGM has no unmodeled frailty, the Cox HR within simulations targets the correct conditional parameter, while the AHR provides the appropriate marginal summary for real-data applications where unmeasured heterogeneity may exist.

5.4 Cox HR as an Operational Tool for Subgroup Selection

In the ForestSearch workflow, per-replicate Cox HR estimates and thresholds (e.g., β̂ ≥ log (0.90)) serve as operational selection criteria for identifying candidate subgroups. This is distinct from their role as causal estimands. The recent literature (Edelmann 2025; Martinussen 2022; Fay & Li 2024) notes that the instantaneous Cox HR at time t is a causal effect for the baseline population but not necessarily for the population at risk at t.

Within the ForestSearch framework, this distinction is addressed as follows:

-   The true treatment effects are defined through ψ⁰(z), a baseline causal quantity; the AHR and CDE summarize it over a region and are the truths a simulation plants.
-   Subgroup selection ranks candidates on the within-subgroup Cox coefficient β̂(g), and the reported estimand is that coefficient’s least-false value in the selected region, β(Ĥ), corrected for selection by multiplier resampling (León and Anderson 2026a, Sections 1.2 and 3) — taken as the target by institutional choice, with the interpretive limits of a hazard ratio acknowledged (2026a, Section 8).
-   Evaluation in simulations scores bias and coverage against both the planted quantities (AHR, CDE, marginal HR) and β(Ĥ) (2026a, Section 7; Supplementary Section S8); the decomposition of error into subgroup misidentification versus finite-sample noise applies to the planted targets. The bootstrap bias correction of the 2024 workflow (hr.H.bc) is reproduced to first order by the multiplier correction (2026a, Theorem 1).

6 Computation Pipeline

The complete pipeline from model specification to estimand computation is summarized below.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Step  Operation                  Formula
  ------ -------------------------- -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    1    Fit Weibull AFT            log T_(i) = μ + X_(i)^(′)γ + σε_(i)

    2    Hazard-scale transform     β₀ =  − γ/σ

    3    Individual log-hazards     θ_(i)(x) = X_(i)(x)^(′)β₀

    4    Individual causal log-HR   loghr_po_(i) = θ_(i)(1) − θ_(i)(0)

    5a   AHR (subgroup 𝒮)           $\text{AHR}(\mathcal{S}) = {\exp}\left( {\overline{\text{loghr\_po}}}_{\mathcal{S}} \right)$

    5b   CDE (subgroup 𝒮)           $\text{CDE}(\mathcal{S}) = {\overline{{\exp}(\theta_{1})}}_{\mathcal{S}}\;/\;{\overline{{\exp}(\theta_{0})}}_{\mathcal{S}}$

    5c   Marginal HR (subgroup 𝒮)   exp (β̂_(Cox)) from stacked potential outcomes
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In ForestSearch, Steps 1–4 are performed by generate_aft_dgm_flex(), which stores β₀ in dgm$model_params$b0 and the individual-level quantities (loghr_po, lin_pred_0, lin_pred_1) in dgm$df_super. Steps 5a–5b are computed by cox_ahr_cde_analysis(); Step 5c is computed within calculate_hazard_ratios().
